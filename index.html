Cloud Computing and Security Issues


          
          

            
            

              
Most organisations don't buy their own hardware anymore. They rent storage, software and
                processing power and pay only for what they use. It's cheaper, and they can scale up or down whenever
                they need to. Even small businesses can now run on the same computing power as large ones without paying
                to build it.

            

          


          
The trade-off is control. Data that used to sit inside a company's own building now sits on
            infrastructure the company doesn't own and can't fully see. That's why the biggest objection to moving to the
            cloud isn't cost or speed. It's security.

Data Breach


          
Data breaches rank among the most damaging security threats in cloud computing. The Target
            attack shows the
            scale of exposure possible. Attackers entered through a third-party HVAC vendor, not Target's own systems,
            and exposed roughly 70 million customer records. The company paid close to $400 million in the aftermath
            (Jathanna & Jagli, 2017).


          
The entry point matters more than people assume. Any vendor connected to a network becomes
            part of that
            network's attack surface, whether the organisation accounts for it or not.


          
Cloud platforms amplify this risk. Large amounts of sensitive data sit on shared servers,
            so one successful
            breach can affect many users at once (Dawood et al., 2023). A single company losing a server room loses its
            own records. A compromised cloud platform can expose everyone on it.


          
Hashizume et al. (2013, p. 8) define the core problem directly: data leakage happens when
            data reaches the
            wrong hands during transfer, storage, audit, or processing. Moving data into the cloud multiplies the points
            where it can leak, and usually reduces how much of that movement an organisation can actually see.

Identity and Access Management


          
Weak identity and access management lets unauthorised actors exploit cloud systems, often
            without
            triggering any alarm. Khalil et al. (2014) group these failures into four categories: account and service
            hijacking, malicious insiders, weak authentication, and poor handling of privileged accounts.


          
A stolen password grants an attacker the exact same access as the legitimate employee. The
            system does not
            flag the intrusion, because it reads the attacker as a normal user. Confidentiality, integrity, and
            availability all fail at once.


          
Phishing remains the most common route to stolen credentials. The weakness sits with
            people, not
            technology, which is an uncomfortable finding for organisations that assume more security tools will fix the
            problem.


          
Alghofaili et al. (2021) point to a separate issue: infrastructure changes fast under IaaS
            elasticity, and
            access controls set up once quickly go stale. Shared multi-tenant resources compound this, letting
            authenticated users reach data they were never meant to see. Jansen and Grance (2011, p. 15) put it plainly:
            without governance, an organisation's own policies and procedures for privacy and security get overlooked,
            putting the organisation at risk. Access management is not a one-time setup. It needs continuous attention.

Malware and Ransomware


          
Cloud infrastructure and the end users who depend on it are both increasingly targeted by
            malware and
            ransomware. Spark, a New Zealand telecommunications provider, lost service in September 2014 after attackers
            installed malware on end-user devices and used them to launch a denial-of-service attack against Spark's
            cloud services. Spark denied any fault in its own architecture, placing the blame on the infected end-user
            devices instead (Ahmed & Litchfield, 2018).


          
Penjala (2019, p. 2) frames information security as a continuous race, not a fixed target:
            as threat actors
            evolve their techniques, organisations need matching effort from security managers, IT specialists, and
            ordinary staff, not just one department.


          
Dawood et al. (2023, p. 1) list the typical risk profile cloud computing faces: data abuse,
            malicious
            insiders, insecure interfaces, shared technology weaknesses, data loss, and hijacking. Malware rarely acts
            alone. It typically exploits a weak account or a careless user, tying this issue directly back to the first
            two.
